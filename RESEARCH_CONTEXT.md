# Research context and closing remark

Updated 7 November 2026.

**This is currently an explanatory mathematical note: a rethinking and illustration of established congestion and information-externality research. An original research contribution has not been established.** The broad idea that common, individually attractive advice can worsen collective outcomes is already in the literature. Recasting it in terms of AI agents does not itself make it new.

The note derives explicit formulas, finite-population comparisons, and a Bayesian implementation result within its own model. Their originality relative to existing theorems remains unverified. Correct algebra, reproducible computations, and polished presentation do not establish novelty. The paper should be shared as a working note inviting scrutiny, rather than a newly discovered paradox or a measured economic effect of AI.

## Foundations and closest research

- Braess, Nagurney and Wakolbinger (2005), **On a Paradox of Traffic Planning**. English translation of Braess's 1968 work. Equilibrium deterioration after adding a route. https://doi.org/10.1287/trsc.1050.0127
- Acemoglu, Makhdoumi, Malekian and Ozdaglar (2018), **Informational Braess' Paradox: The Effect of Information on Traffic Congestion**. A direct precedent for harmful expansions of route information. https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.2017.1712
- Jon Kleinberg and Manish Raghavan (2021), **Algorithmic Monoculture and Social Welfare**. Establishes that a more accurate common ranking algorithm can reduce collective performance. This already supplies the central shared-algorithm intuition. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2018340118
- Baek, Bastani and Chen (first posted 2025; revised 2026), **Strategic Hiring under Algorithmic Monoculture**. Strategic responses and congestion-aware differentiation make the allowed behavior important. https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20063v2
- Robert Kleinberg, Erald Sinanaj and Éva Tardos (2026), **Price of Anarchy of Algorithmic Monoculture**. Generalizes the earlier matching-market model and bounds its welfare loss. Found in the later novelty check; absent from the earlier manuscript bibliography. Its model and proofs require full-text comparison before asserting originality. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00444
- Feng Chi and Nathan Yang (2026), **Shared Intelligence and Misaligned Firm Entry**. Especially close framing: precision benefits, dependence costs and endogenous adoption. The indexed abstract was consulted; full-text theorem overlap remains unresolved. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6799682
- Chiang et al. (2026), **Personalized Recommendations Without Inducing Congestion: Mitigating Disparities in the NYC High School Match**. Recommendations as market-shaping interventions in a capacity-constrained application. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08275

## The film analogy

The bar scene in *A Beautiful Mind* illustrates crowding around a preferred option, but its proposed arrangement is not generally a Nash equilibrium: a person may profitably deviate if everyone else avoids that option. James Brander discusses the issue in his 2003 review in the Canadian Journal of Economics: https://blogs.ubc.ca/jbrander/files/2016/01/Beautiful-Mind-Review.pdf . The scene is an intuition, not a formal foundation for this note.
